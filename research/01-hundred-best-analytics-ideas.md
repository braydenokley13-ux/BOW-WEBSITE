# The 100 Best Analytics Ideas

**Purpose:** a ranked bank of the sharpest concepts in sports analytics and sports
business, translated for a platform whose whole premise is *teach the front-office
mental model through real decisions*. Every idea below is scored on two axes Bow
should always ask about a concept before building around it — **does a fan already
get this?** and **does an executive actually use this?** — and closes with the
"Bow's Edge": the specific teaching move that turns jargon into an aha moment.

Ranking logic: concepts are ordered within ten clusters by *teachability × leverage*
— how much explanatory power a reader gets per unit of new vocabulary. Cluster order
itself runs from "closest to what Bow already ships" (player/contract valuation,
where AASV already lives) to "furthest out" (league-level economics), so the
implementation model can read top-to-bottom as a build-order signal, not just a
popularity ranking.

Legend: **Fan** = does the median sports fan already have an intuition for this
without training? **Exec** = do real front offices visibly act on this (not just
discuss it)? Both rated Low / Med / High.

## Cluster A — Player Valuation & Aging (#1–10)

| # | Concept | What it is & why it matters | Fan | Exec | Bow's Edge |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Surplus value** | Production value minus true cost; the single number that unifies "is this a good player" and "is this a good contract" into one line. It's the atomic unit of front-office thinking — everything else in this list is either an input to it or a use of it. | Low | High | Bow already ships this as AASV. Never show the number alone — always show the two bars it's the *gap between* (production bar, cost bar), so the surplus is visibly the whitespace, not an abstraction. |
| 2 | **Replacement level** | The performance a team could get for free (minimum-salary/waiver-wire talent). Value is measured *above* this floor, not from zero — a stat line that looks good in isolation can be replacement-level or worse once you subtract what a bench guy already gives you. | Low | High | Anchor every valuation chart with a visible "replacement line" — a dotted line at league-minimum production — so surplus reads as "how far above the free option," not an arbitrary score. |
| 3 | **Aging curve** | The typical rise-peak-decline shape of performance by age and position; front offices price *future* production, not past. A 29-year-old signing a 5-year deal is really buying years 29–33, and year 33 is worth much less than year 29. | Med (fans sense "he's getting old") | High | Show the curve as a literal hill under every long-term contract: shade the years already banked green, the years still owed under the descending part of the curve red. |
| 4 | **Positional scarcity** | Not all wins cost the same — some positions/skills (rim protection, elite QB, left-handed reliever who can get out lefties) are scarcer in the market and price higher per unit of production than the raw stat implies. | Low | High | A "market price per marginal win, by position" mini-table next to any comp — shows *why* a mediocre center costs more than a very good bench wing. |
| 5 | **Marginal wins → win curve** | The relationship between roster quality and actual wins is nonlinear; each additional marginal win is worth more once a team is near a playoff/title threshold than when it's already bad or already great. | Low | High | An interactive curve: drag a "current win total" slider and watch the dollar-value of the *next* marginal win rise and fall — this alone teaches why contenders overpay and rebuilders don't. |
| 6 | **Availability / durability value** | Games played is itself a skill front offices price — a slightly-worse player who plays 82 games is often worth more than a superstar who plays 55, because a bench replacement fills the empty games at replacement level, not superstar level. | Med | High | Show "value per game" next to "value per season" — the gap between them *is* the durability discount, made visible instead of asserted. |
| 7 | **Redundancy / fit discount** | Two players with overlapping skill sets are worth less together than the sum of their standalone value (three non-shooting bigs; two ball-dominant guards). Roster construction is a fit problem, not a talent-accumulation problem. | Low | Med | A "skill overlap" heatmap across a roster — bright cells where two players compete for the same touches/possessions/innings. |
| 8 | **Peak-age windows by position** | Peak age itself varies by position/role (NBA bigs often peak later than guards; MLB power peaks earlier than contact/plate discipline) — a single league-wide aging curve is a simplification that misprices specific archetypes. | Low | Med | Small multiples: the same aging-curve chart faceted by position, so the shape difference *is* the insight. |
| 9 | **Tail risk vs. median projection** | Two players can share a median projection but differ wildly in how bad their 10th-percentile outcome is — front offices price the distribution, not just the expected value, especially for aging or oft-injured players. | Low | Med | Show a projection as a range (10th–90th percentile band), not a point estimate — and let users see how a "safe" vs. "boom/bust" player can carry the same median. |
| 10 | **Prospect hit-rate tables** | Empirical tables of "what fraction of players picked at slot N ever became an above-average starter" — the single most sobering dataset in sports, because it quantifies just how much of the draft is educated guessing. | Med | High | A hit-rate table the reader can filter by position/sport, paired with "your team's actual pick" so the abstract percentage becomes a specific gut-check. |

## Cluster B — Contract & Cap Mechanics (#11–20)

| # | Concept | What it is & why it matters | Fan | Exec | Bow's Edge |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 11 | **Contract as a financial instrument** | An NBA/NFL contract behaves like a bond (guaranteed money = fixed coupon) blended with an option (team/player options = embedded calls/puts) — reframing a "deal" as an instrument is the single biggest unlock for making cap strategy legible. | Low | High | Literally label a contract's option year "Team Call Option — team can decline to pay" the first time a reader hits one; the finance vocabulary teaches itself once used correctly once. |
| 12 | **Time value of cap dollars** | A dollar of cap space this year is worth more than a dollar next year if you're in a competitive window now — front-loaded vs. back-loaded deals are a bet on when the team will need flexibility most. | Low | Med | A payroll timeline chart (already speced in Deliverable 7) with a shaded "current window" band — front/back-loading becomes visibly a bet on where the money lands relative to the shaded band. |
| 13 | **Apron / luxury-tax mechanics** | Spending thresholds that don't just cost money (tax) but remove *tools* (trade exceptions, mid-level exception, ability to aggregate salary) — the real punishment is lost optionality, not the tax bill. | Low | High | A "what this team can no longer do" checklist that lights up red as a slider crosses each apron line — optionality loss made concrete. |
| 14 | **Options as embedded derivatives** | Player/team options, and their more complex cousins (early termination options, non-guaranteed years) are literally call/put options on a player's own performance — whoever holds the option captures the upside/downside asymmetry. | Low | Med | Frame every option year with "who benefits if he's great? who benefits if he's hurt?" — two-sentence framing that makes the asymmetry obvious without the word "derivative." |
| 15 | **Dead cap as depreciation** | Money still owed to a player no longer on the roster (post-trade, post-waive) — economically identical to writing off a bad asset; teams "pay for the mistake twice" (once in wins lost, once in cap dollars that buy nothing). | Med | High | Show dead cap as a literal gray/hatched bar sitting *inside* the payroll chart, contributing to the cap number but producing zero win value — visually distinct from productive spend. |
| 16 | **Restricted free agency & poison-pill offers** | An offer sheet structured (front-loaded, oddly shaped bonuses) specifically to be hard for the original team to match — a mini hostile-takeover-defense problem borrowed straight from M&A. | Low | Med | A "would the original team match this?" mini-decision game — reader designs an offer sheet, sees whether the incumbent's cap situation lets them match. |
| 17 | **Bird rights / cap exceptions as optionality** | Retention rights and exceptions (MLE, bi-annual, disabled player) are tools that only have value in the right situation — they're optionality a team banks by *not* using cap space today. | Low | Med | "Tools in the toolbox" inventory per team — a simple badge row showing which exceptions a team currently holds and hasn't spent. |
| 18 | **Guaranteed vs. non-guaranteed money as insurance** | Guarantee structure is literally a risk-transfer instrument: guaranteed money insures the player against being cut; non-guaranteed protects the team against a decline no one saw coming. | Low | Med | Two-color stacked bar (guaranteed vs. team-protected) on every contract card — reframes "3 years, $60M" into "how much risk each side actually carries." |
| 19 | **Competitive balance tax as a progressive tax bracket** | Baseball's CBT (and NBA's aprons) function like income tax brackets — marginal spending above a threshold is taxed at escalating rates, and repeater penalties compound like a second bracket for repeat offenders. | Low | Med | A literal tax-bracket visual (like a paycheck tax breakdown) instead of a single scary "luxury tax bill" number. |
| 20 | **Trade exceptions as a shelf-life option** | A trade exception (cash/expiring player sent out, nothing matching salary back) is a one-year option to absorb salary later — it expires, so an unused exception is value evaporating in real time. | Low | Med | A literal countdown badge ("expires in 47 days") on any trade-exception mention — urgency as a teaching device. |

## Cluster C — Team Portfolio & Roster Construction (#21–30)

| # | Concept | What it is & why it matters | Fan | Exec | Bow's Edge |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 21 | **Roster as an asset portfolio** | The master metaphor: a roster is a portfolio of assets (players, picks, exceptions, cap space) with different risk/return/liquidity profiles — every front-office decision is a portfolio rebalancing decision. | Low | High | Bow's core "front office" framing already gestures at this — make it literal with a portfolio pie (stars / role players / picks / cap space / dead weight) per team. |
| 22 | **Correlated roster risk** | Building a roster of similar-skill, similar-age, similar-injury-history players concentrates risk the way an undiversified stock portfolio does — if the shared risk factor hits (age, style of play, injury type), it hits everyone at once. | Low | Med | A "correlation" flag: highlight when 3+ rotation players share an injury-prone profile or an aging-out timeline simultaneously. |
| 23 | **Star + role-player barbell** | The dominant modern construction strategy: pay stars true market price, fill around them with cheap, replaceable, high-floor role players rather than a roster of expensive "pretty good" players — the "avoid the middle class" thesis. | Med | High | A payroll-vs-production scatter plot with a visible "middle class trap" zone — teams whose $15–25M players plot below the surplus line. |
| 24 | **Two-timelines problem** | When a team's win-now assets (aging stars) and its future assets (young players/picks) don't share a competitive window, every trade forces a choice about *which timeline* the front office is actually building for. | Med | High | A literal two-lane timeline chart per roster — "assets useful 2026-27" vs. "assets useful 2029+" — makes an incoherent roster visually incoherent. |
| 25 | **Sunk cost in extension decisions** | The temptation to keep paying/playing a declining former star because of what he's already been paid or done, rather than what he's projected to produce going forward — the single most common front-office bias. | High (fans feel this too) | High | A "forget what you paid" toggle on player cards that hides past salary and shows only forward-looking projection — literally operationalizes ignoring sunk cost. |
| 26 | **Opportunity cost of cap space/roster spots** | Every dollar or roster spot committed to Player A is a dollar/spot *not* available for Player B — cap space and roster spots are the scarce resource, not money in the abstract. | Med | High | "What else this could buy" — next to any big signing, show 2-3 alternative uses of that same cap number (a role player + pick vs. one star). |
| 27 | **Tanking as a real option** | Deliberately accepting worse outcomes now (losing) to preserve or increase the value of a future option (higher draft odds) — economically identical to a company delaying earnings to invest in R&D. | High (fans already argue about this) | High | A literal "value of losing" calculator: given a team's record, show expected lottery odds and expected draft-slot surplus value, so tanking becomes a number, not a vibe. |
| 28 | **Buyout market as distressed-asset arbitrage** | Players released from guaranteed money re-enter the market at a discount because the paying team already ate the cost — contenders "buy the distressed asset" cheap since someone else already wrote off the loss. | Low | Med | A "who's paying for this" split bar: original team's dead money vs. new team's actual cost — shows the arbitrage explicitly. |
| 29 | **Salary matching as deal structuring** | Multi-team trade salary-matching rules force the same creative structuring problem as M&A deal-making (which vehicle carries which liability) — a trade is closer to a financing transaction than a swap. | Low | Med | An interactive "does this trade work" matcher — reader drags players/picks between teams and gets real-time legality feedback against real cap rules. |
| 30 | **Expiring contracts as liquidity** | An expiring, non-guaranteed-beyond-this-year contract is the most liquid asset in a cap system — easy to trade, easy to absorb, easy to walk away from — liquidity itself has value independent of the player's talent. | Low | Med | Badge contracts by "years of commitment remaining" the way a bond is labeled by maturity — teaches liquidity as a property separate from quality. |

## Cluster D — Draft & Prospect Valuation (#31–40)

| # | Concept | What it is & why it matters | Fan | Exec | Bow's Edge |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 31 | **Draft pick value charts** | Point-value tables assigning each draft slot an expected worth (NFL's Jimmy Johnson chart and its modern analytics-era revisions) — the backbone of every trade-up/trade-down negotiation. | Med (NFL fans know the chart exists) | High | An interactive chart calculator: drag picks between two teams, see the value gap in real time, exactly like the real war-room tool GMs use. |
| 32 | **Draft slot surplus-value curve** | Expected production minus rookie-scale cost, by pick number — early picks aren't just "better players," they're better players on *much* cheaper contracts, which is why draft capital is so prized even beyond the talent. | Low | High | Overlay two curves — "expected talent" and "expected surplus value" — so the reader sees the curves diverge (surplus falls off even faster than talent). |
| 33 | **Power-law upside in prospect grading** | Draft outcomes follow a venture-capital-style power law — a handful of picks generate almost all the value, most picks bust or become replacement-level — so a scouting philosophy of "swing for aces" can beat "avoid busts" late in a draft. | Low | Med | A "outcome distribution" histogram by round showing the long tail — visually explains why teams keep taking boom/bust fliers in rounds 2+. |
| 34 | **Floor vs. ceiling tradeoff** | Two prospects can have the same expected value while one is a "safe" high-floor/low-ceiling bet and the other a volatile high-ceiling/low-floor bet — the right choice depends on the team's win-now vs. rebuild context (cluster F). | Med | High | A floor/ceiling range bar per prospect, plus a toggle: "if you're rebuilding" vs. "if you're contending," re-sorting the big board by which archetype fits the context. |
| 35 | **Positional value in draft capital** | Some positions are worth more draft capital because the position itself carries a scarcity premium in the pro game (elite QB, shutdown corner, rim-protecting big) — teams should and do pay a positional premium in the draft, not just for talent. | Low | Med | A "positional premium" multiplier shown next to raw prospect grade — same grade, different pick-value depending on position. |
| 36 | **Trade-up/trade-down NPV** | Moving up costs surplus value (you're trading multiple cheap assets for one, at a markup); moving down banks surplus value but adds risk (more picks, more variance) — a genuine expected-value calculation, not just gut feel. | Low | Med | A trade calculator with an explicit "expected surplus gained/lost" output number, not just "pick value traded." |
| 37 | **Combine/measurable predictive power (or lack thereof)** | Athletic testing numbers correlate weakly with pro success for most positions/skills — a case study in how "objective-looking" data can still be a weak signal, useful for teaching *quality* of evidence, not just *presence* of data. | Med | Med | A correlation scatter (40-time vs. career AV, vertical vs. NBA win shares) that lets the reader see the weak/no relationship themselves rather than being told. |
| 38 | **Age-adjusted prospect grading** | A draft-eligible player's stats mean something different at 19 vs. 22 — performing well against younger competition, or performing only adequately against older competition, changes the read entirely. | Low | Med | Show "age relative to draft class" as a visible modifier on every prospect stat line, the way baseball scouting reports already do with age-vs-level. |
| 39 | **Draft-and-stash as long-dated options** | Draft rights held on a player who won't play domestically for years (international draft-and-stash, baseball's international bonus pool prospects) are long-dated call options — cheap now, potentially valuable later, with a long time horizon that most fans don't track. | Low | Low | A "years until this option can be exercised" countdown — same device as the trade-exception shelf-life, reused for pattern recognition. |
| 40 | **Lottery odds as an expected-value gamble** | Draft lottery reform (flattened odds, play-in tournaments) is itself a designed gambling mechanism — understanding the odds structure explains why tanking incentives shift when leagues change the rules. | Med | Med | A "if the league changed the odds to X, how would tanking incentives change" interactive slider — ties directly into #27. |

## Cluster E — Trade Mechanics & Market Structure (#41–50)

| # | Concept | What it is & why it matters | Fan | Exec | Bow's Edge |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 41 | **Trade liquidity** | Not every asset is equally tradeable — no-trade clauses, unusual contract structures, or a thin market of interested teams make an asset "illiquid" even if its on-paper value is high. | Low | High | A "how many teams could realistically absorb this contract" counter — turns an abstract concept into a literal number. |
| 42 | **Information asymmetry** | Teams possess private information (medicals, locker-room dynamics, scheme fit) that the market (other teams, media, fans) doesn't have — the best-informed team in a trade usually wins it. | Med | High | An explicit "what the selling team knows that we don't" callout box on trade retrospectives — teaches humility about public analysis. |
| 43 | **Third-team facilitation** | A team with cap space or trade exceptions but no direct interest in either principal player can insert itself into a trade purely to extract a fee (a pick, a swap) — literally an intermediary/broker role. | Low | Med | Model a 3-team trade as a literal flow diagram with a "toll" arrow into the third team — makes the broker role visible instead of buried in trade-machine text. |
| 44 | **No-trade clause as illiquidity premium** | A no-trade clause is a concession a team pays for (in dollars or years) that later removes their own optionality — the player, not the team, holds the option to block. | Low | Med | Same "who holds the option" framing as #14, applied to movement rights instead of salary. |
| 45 | **Deadline market clearing** | Trade deadlines create an artificial liquidity event — the same asset trades for a different price the week before vs. the week after, because the *option to wait* expires at a hard date. | Med | Med | A "price of a rental player over time" chart showing value decay as the deadline approaches then a cliff afterward. |
| 46 | **Combinatorial trade matching** | Multi-team, multi-asset trades are a matching problem (who wants what, who can absorb what) with combinatorial complexity — small rule changes (e.g., aggregation limits) can make or break entire trade classes. | Low | Med | An interactive trade-builder sandbox testing real cap rules as constraints — the "does this work" tool doubles as a lesson in combinatorics. |
| 47 | **Buyer's vs. seller's market at the deadline** | League-wide competitive balance (how many teams are "in it") swings deadline prices — a deadline with 20 teams thinking they can contend is a seller's market; a deadline with 8 clear sellers and 6 buyers is a buyer's market. | Med | Med | A league-wide "buyers vs. sellers" gauge updated through the season, contextualizing why the same player fetches a different price in different years. |
| 48 | **Contract as a poison pill in trades** | A recently-re-signed player often can't be traded for months (rules exist specifically to prevent value-manipulation), turning a "great contract" into a temporarily untradeable asset — timing restrictions matter as much as value. | Low | Med | A "tradeable as of" date badge on recently-signed players. |
| 49 | **Salary-dump as balance-sheet cleanup** | Attaching a pick/young asset to an unwanted contract to get a team to "take back money" is functionally identical to paying a buyer to take a bad loan off your balance sheet. | Low | Med | Show the "sweetener" attached to a dump trade as an explicit price tag: "it cost X draft value to remove this contract." |
| 50 | **Return package construction** | Constructing a competitive trade offer optimizes across value, fit, and the other team's constraints simultaneously — not just "who offers the most value" but "whose offer actually solves the other GM's problem." | Low | Med | A "does this solve their problem" checklist alongside the standard "value" comparison in any trade proposal tool. |

## Cluster F — Competitive Windows & Timing (#51–60)

| # | Concept | What it is & why it matters | Fan | Exec | Bow's Edge |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 51 | **Championship window** | The span of years during which a roster's talent, health, and cap situation align well enough to realistically contend — the central timing concept that determines almost every other decision (buy, sell, hold, extend). | High | High | Already speced as a core embed (Deliverable 7) — render as a literal shaded band across a multi-year timeline, with team payroll and star-age curves overlaid. |
| 52 | **Contend / sustain / reload / rebuild framework** | A four-stage lifecycle model front offices use to self-classify and set strategy — the label changes what "a good trade" even means for that team this year. | Med | High | A single-word team status badge (like a stock ticker's buy/hold/sell) computed from roster age curve + cap flexibility + recent performance. |
| 53 | **Marginal cost per marginal win, by win total** | The cost of adding one more win rises steeply as a team approaches the playoff/title threshold — cheap wins exist at 25-win and 65-win teams; expensive wins exist at the 48-52 win "fighting for seeding" tier. | Low | High | A cost curve (dollars per marginal win, x-axis = current win total) — directly explains why bad and great teams both find bargains that middling teams can't. |
| 54 | **Rebuild vs. retool vs. reload decision tree** | A structured framework for choosing between a full teardown, a partial reshuffle, or an aggressive current-core push — the actual decision GMs face isn't binary, it's a tree with distinct branches and triggers. | Med | High | Deliverable 4 already proposes an interactive version of this as a "decision challenge" — directly reuse the same tree as an explainer graphic. |
| 55 | **Asset accumulation vs. deployment phase** | Front offices cycle between phases of *gathering* optionality (picks, exceptions, cap space) and phases of *spending* it — reading which phase a team is in explains moves that look passive or aggressive in isolation. | Low | Med | A phase indicator ("accumulating" / "deploying") per team, computed from trade activity + asset inventory trend over the last 12-24 months. |
| 56 | **Expected value of a title shot** | Because titles are rare and binary, the "value" of a contending season should be discounted by title probability, not treated as a guaranteed payoff — a 15%-to-win-it-all season is worth roughly 15% of a title, not a title. | Low | Med | A "title equity" number (season value × title probability) next to raw playoff odds — reframes hype into expected value. |
| 57 | **Buy-or-sell calculus at the deadline** | A structured comparison of (marginal title-probability gain from buying) × (title's dollar/prestige value) vs. (cost in future assets) — the actual math underlying "are we buyers or sellers" chatter. | Med | High | A literal calculator: slide "current title odds" and "asset cost," output "was this trade worth it" — ties #56 and #45 together into one tool. |
| 58 | **Aging-cliff timing risk** | Windows close faster than intuition suggests because decline is often nonlinear (a plateau, then a cliff) rather than gradual — the riskiest mistake is assuming one more "normal" year when a player is at the edge of the cliff. | Med | High | Overlay real historical aging-cliff examples (not hypothetical) on the aging-curve chart from #3 — pattern recognition from real cases beats an abstract curve. |
| 59 | **Competitive symmetry across a division/conference** | A team's own window is only half the picture — its odds also depend on whether rivals are rebuilding or peaking at the same time; the same roster is worth more in a weak conference-cycle than a stacked one. | Low | Med | A "relative window" chart comparing a team's win-curve trajectory against its 3-4 closest competitors on the same axes. |
| 60 | **Optionality of "wait and see"** | Sometimes the highest-value move is deliberately not deciding yet — holding a trade chip, a coaching decision, or a roster spot open preserves the option value of better information arriving later. | Low | Med | Frame explicitly as "what does waiting cost, and what does it buy" — a two-column comparison rather than treating inaction as a non-decision. |

## Cluster G — Risk, Variance & Insurance (#61–70)

| # | Concept | What it is & why it matters | Fan | Exec | Bow's Edge |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 61 | **Injury-risk pricing by age/position/history** | Actuarial-style tables of games-missed probability by player profile — front offices (and increasingly, real insurers) price a player's health risk almost like an underwriter prices a policy. | Low | High | A "games-missed distribution" histogram per player archetype, echoing #9's range framing — risk as a distribution, not a rumor. |
| 62 | **Variance budget in roster construction** | A team can deliberately choose a higher-variance roster (several boom/bust bets) or a lower-variance one (all solid floors) depending on whether it needs to gamble for outcomes above its median (a bad team needing a miracle) or protect a strong median (a favorite). | Low | Med | A variance dial next to the standard value estimate — same median outcome, different spread, framed as a strategic choice not a flaw. |
| 63 | **Real contract insurance** | Teams (and increasingly individual contracts) literally buy insurance policies against career-ending injury on large deals — an underused, very real financial mechanism that maps risk transfer directly onto sports. | Low | Med | A short explainer sidebar the first time a "why would they guarantee that much" question comes up — real-world grounding beats a hypothetical. |
| 64 | **Regression to the mean as a valuation tool** | A player's hot/cold stretch is partly signal, partly noise — regression tells you how much of a breakout or slump to actually believe before pricing an extension or a sell-high trade. | Med | High | A "how much of this is real" split bar (skill vs. variance) computed from a simple regression model on recent vs. career performance. |
| 65 | **Small-sample-size traps** | Fans and even front offices routinely overreact to a hot month, a great playoff series, or a strong combine — recognizing sample size is the single highest-leverage "think like an analyst" skill to teach. | High (fans intuitively distrust "small sample" when told, but rarely self-catch it) | High | A visible "sample size" badge (games/PA/possessions) attached to every hot stat, plus a "how confident should you be" translation in plain English. |
| 66 | **Correlation risk across a roster's health** | If half a rotation shares an injury type or an age-driven decline pattern, an "unlucky year" isn't independent bad luck across players — it's one risk factor hitting multiple exposures at once, exactly like a concentrated stock position. | Low | Med | Reuse #22's correlation flag but framed through the injury lens specifically — same visual device, different trigger. |
| 67 | **Force-majeure / uncontrollable risk (lockouts, pandemics, rule changes)** | Some risks are outside any front office's model — legal, macro, or rule-change risk that resets the value of every asset simultaneously — worth naming explicitly so users don't over-trust any model's precision. | Med | Med | An explicit "what this model can't see" disclosure attached to every projection tool — honesty about model limits builds more trust than false precision. |
| 68 | **Makeup/character risk (and its measurement problem)** | Off-court/off-field risk is real and priced by teams, but it's the hardest input to quantify honestly and the easiest to smuggle bias into — a good teaching opportunity about the limits of quantification itself. | Med | Med | Treat carefully and narrowly: name it as a real front-office input without attempting to "score" it numerically — teach the concept, don't build a flawed metric. |
| 69 | **System/scheme-fit risk** | A player's value is partly conditional on coaching scheme and teammates — trading for a great player who doesn't fit the new team's system is a well-documented, recurring failure mode. | Med | Med | A "fit score" callout on cross-team comparisons — explicitly separate "how good is he" from "how good is he *here*." |
| 70 | **Clutch performance and its regression** | "Clutch" stats are usually driven by small samples and regress hard — a great teaching case for #64/#65 using a topic fans are emotionally invested in and eager to argue about. | High | Med | A specific, named "clutch vs. career" comparison tool for a handful of famous "clutch" reputations — controversy as a teaching hook. |

## Cluster H — Advanced On-Field Metrics (#71–80)

| # | Concept | What it is & why it matters | Fan | Exec | Bow's Edge |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 71 | **Plus-minus-derived impact metrics (RAPM/EPM/RAPTOR-family)** | Metrics that isolate a player's effect on scoring margin while adjusting for teammates/opponents — the backbone of modern basketball evaluation, and a great case study in "why box-score stats alone mislead." | Low | High | Show box-score value next to impact-metric value for the same player, side by side, so the *gap* between them becomes the lesson. |
| 72 | **EPA (Expected Points Added)** | Football's foundational modern metric: every play's value measured against the expected points of the situation it created, replacing "yards" as the unit of offensive/defensive value. | Low | High | A single explainer: "a 4-yard gain on 3rd-and-3 is worth more than a 4-yard gain on 3rd-and-12" — one sentence, permanently reusable. |
| 73 | **Stuff+/Pitch-modeling metrics** | Baseball's granular pitch-level models (movement, velocity, release point) predicting outcome quality independent of results — a case study in measuring *process* over *outcome*. | Low | High | Explicitly contrast "results-based" (ERA) vs. "process-based" (Stuff+) for the same pitcher over a small sample — teaches the process/outcome distinction generally. |
| 74 | **Tracking-data derived metrics (speed, distance, closeout quality)** | Second Spectrum/NGS-style optical/radar tracking turns motion itself into data — separates "what happened" from "how it happened physically," enabling things like defensive positioning grades that box scores can't see. | Low | High | A short "how this is measured" explainer any time a tracking-derived stat appears — demystifying the sensor layer builds trust in the number. |
| 75 | **Play-type efficiency (Synergy-style)** | Breaking performance down by the specific type of possession/play (isolation, spot-up, post-up, screen types) rather than aggregate stats — reveals *how* a player creates value, useful for fit and scheme questions. | Low | High | A play-type breakdown radar/bar chart per player, directly answering "what is he actually good at" beyond a single efficiency number. |
