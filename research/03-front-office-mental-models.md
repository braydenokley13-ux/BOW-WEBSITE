# Front Office Mental Models

**Purpose:** the reasoning patterns elite front offices actually use, translated
into language a fan (or a middle/high-school student — Bow's core educational
audience) can internalize and reuse outside of sports entirely. This is the
deliverable most directly aligned with Bow's mission statement ("students learn
economics, finance, leadership, and strategy by making the same decisions that
shape teams, leagues, and the business of sports") — every model below should be
read as a transferable life-skill wearing a sports costume.

Each model: **the front-office version → the general principle → the fan
translation → a concrete Bow teaching hook.**

---

## 1. Asset Management

**Front office:** every player, pick, exception, and cap dollar is an asset with
a distinct risk/return/liquidity profile that must be actively managed, not just
accumulated.

**General principle:** the same logic underlies personal finance (your time,
money, and skills are assets to allocate, not just resources to spend) and
corporate strategy (a company's product lines, IP, and talent are a managed
portfolio).

**Fan translation:** "your roster isn't a list of good players, it's a set of
things you own that you have to actively decide what to do with."

**Bow hook:** frame the whole "front office" product surface explicitly as
portfolio management — literally reuse personal-finance vocabulary (assets,
allocation, rebalancing) rather than sports-only vocabulary, so the transfer to
"this is just how you should think about your own life" is explicit, not implied.

---

## 2. Opportunity Cost

**Front office:** the true cost of any roster/cap decision is the best alternative
forgone, not the sticker price — signing Player A costs both his salary *and* the
best player you could have signed instead.

**General principle:** the foundational concept of economics; nearly every
personal and business decision is mispriced by people who only count the direct
cost and ignore the foregone alternative.

**Fan translation:** "what else could this money/roster spot have bought?"

**Bow hook:** attach an explicit "what else this could buy" comparison (Deliverable
1, #26) to every major transaction shown on the platform — never show a cost in
isolation.

---

## 3. Marginal Thinking

**Front office:** decisions are made at the margin — not "is this a good player"
but "is this specific additional player, at this specific additional cost, worth
more than the next-best use of that cost, given what we already have."

**General principle:** marginal analysis (marginal cost vs. marginal benefit) is
the single most-used tool in economics and business decision-making, and the
most commonly skipped step in casual reasoning.

**Fan translation:** "don't ask if he's good — ask if he's good *for what you'd
have to give up to get him, given what you already have.*"

**Bow hook:** the marginal-cost-per-marginal-win curve (Deliverable 1, #53) is the
single cleanest visualization of this idea in sports — make it a recurring visual
motif across the platform, not a one-off chart.

---

## 4. Competitive Windows (Timing Under Constraints)

**Front office:** value isn't static — the same asset is worth more or less
depending on *when* a team can use it relative to its own competitive timeline.

**General principle:** timing-under-constraint shows up everywhere — a startup's
runway, a student's college-application timeline, a career's "prime earning
years" — the general skill is recognizing that resources have a shelf life
relative to your goals, not an absolute value.

**Fan translation:** "the same trade is smart for a team that's about to contend
and dumb for a team that's three years away — timing changes the answer, not just
the number."

**Bow hook:** the championship-window embed (already scoped) should explicitly
prompt "what's your own competitive window for [a goal]?" in educational contexts
— Track 101/201 content is the natural home for this generalization.

---

## 5. Optionality

**Front office:** flexibility itself has value independent of any specific plan —
cap space, trade exceptions, and unattached draft picks are valuable precisely
*because* they're undecided, and using them early destroys that value even if the
specific use looks good in isolation.

**General principle:** the finance concept of an option's value (the right, not
the obligation) generalizes to career decisions (keeping multiple paths open),
negotiation (not committing early), and product strategy (shipping a flexible
platform over a single-purpose tool).

**Fan translation:** "sometimes the smartest move is not making a move yet."

**Bow hook:** Deliverable 1's "toolbox inventory" (#17) and "wait and see"
framing (#60) both operationalize this — the general lesson ("not deciding is
sometimes the correct decision") is one most students have never been explicitly
taught and is a genuine differentiator for Bow's curriculum content.

---

## 6. Diversification vs. Concentration

**Front office:** a roster (or a scouting philosophy, or a team's revenue
streams) can be deliberately diversified to reduce risk, or deliberately
concentrated to maximize a specific bet — neither is universally correct; the
right choice depends on the organization's risk tolerance and situation.

**General principle:** directly maps onto investment portfolio theory
(diversification reduces variance at some cost to expected return) and even
career strategy (a diversified skill set vs. deep specialization).

**Fan translation:** "spreading your bets is safer but caps your upside;
concentrating your bets is riskier but can pay off bigger — good organizations
choose on purpose, they don't drift into it."

**Bow hook:** the "correlated roster risk" concept (Deliverable 1, #22/#66) as a
diversification-failure case study — a strong, concrete on-ramp to teaching
portfolio theory to a student who's never opened a brokerage account.

---

## 7. Leverage

**Front office:** teams use leverage in negotiations (a rival suitor, a deadline,
private information) the same way traders use financial leverage or negotiators
use BATNA (best alternative to a negotiated agreement) — the party with more
credible alternatives extracts a better price.

**General principle:** leverage is the core concept of negotiation theory —
understanding your own and the other party's leverage predicts outcomes better
than understanding "who wants it more."

**Fan translation:** "the team that needs the trade less always gets the better
end of it."

**Bow hook:** frame trade-negotiation content explicitly around "who has leverage
here and why" rather than "who won the trade" — a subtly different and more
teachable question.

---

## 8. Risk-Adjusted Value (Not Just Expected Value)

**Front office:** two assets with the same expected value can carry very
different risk profiles, and a rational front office (like a rational investor)
should price the safer one higher, all else equal — risk itself has a cost.

**General principle:** the Sharpe-ratio intuition from finance (return per unit
of risk, not raw return) applied outside of finance — most people's intuitive
decision-making ignores this and just compares expected outcomes.

**Fan translation:** "a 'guaranteed pretty good' outcome is often worth more than
a 'maybe great, maybe terrible' outcome with the same average" — even though the
average is identical.

**Bow hook:** every projection/valuation tool on the platform should default to
showing a range, never a single point estimate (ties directly to Deliverable 1,
#9 and #61) — this is a design rule as much as an editorial one.

---

## 9. Capital Allocation Discipline

**Front office:** the best organizations have an explicit process (not just a
gut call) for deciding where scarce resources (cap dollars, roster spots, draft
capital) go — discipline about *not* deviating from the model under emotional
pressure (a beloved veteran, a media narrative, a "we have to do something" deadline
itch) is itself a skill.

**General principle:** identical to disciplined investing (not panic-selling,
not chasing a hot stock) — the hardest part of any decision framework is
following it when emotion argues otherwise.

**Fan translation:** "having a plan is easy; sticking to the plan when everyone's
yelling at you to abandon it is the actual skill."

**Bow hook:** a "would you have stuck to the plan" retrospective format (ties to
Editorial Pattern #12, the Report Card) — show a team's stated strategy, then a
tempting distraction, then whether they held discipline.

---

## 10. Incentive Design

**Front office:** how people inside an organization are rewarded (coach bonuses
tied to wins, scouts credited for hits not misses, GMs facing asymmetric
downside from a bad move vs. a good one gone unnoticed) shapes what decisions
actually get made, independent of what the "right" analytical answer is.

**General principle:** Charlie Munger's "show me the incentive and I'll show you
the outcome" — one of the most transferable ideas in this entire document,
applicable to any organization a student will ever work in.

**Fan translation:** "if you want to know why an organization keeps making a
decision that looks wrong from outside, look at who gets rewarded for it."

**Bow hook:** the incentive-misalignment concept (Deliverable 1, #82) paired with
real, named case studies (a coach playing a veteran over a rookie in a lost
season) makes this concrete rather than abstract.

---

## 11. Sunk Cost Discipline

**Front office:** the discipline of evaluating a decision only on its forward-looking
merits, explicitly excluding what's already been spent/committed — the hardest
bias to self-correct because it *feels* like loyalty or consistency rather than a
bias.

**General principle:** universally applicable — continuing a failing project, a
bad relationship, or a declining stock position because of what's "already been
invested" is the single most common decision error across every domain.

**Fan translation:** "what you already paid is gone either way — the only
question is what happens next."

**Bow hook:** the "forget what you paid" toggle (Deliverable 1, #25) is a rare
case where a UI mechanic *is* the lesson — hiding the sunk information and forcing
a forward-only judgment teaches the bias by removing the reader's ability to
commit it.

---

## 12. Process Over Outcome

**Front office:** a good decision can produce a bad outcome (an injury, bad luck)
and a bad decision can produce a good outcome (a lucky bounce) — the best
organizations grade their own decisions on the quality of the process and
information available *at the time*, not on the outcome revealed later.

**General principle:** Annie Duke's poker-derived framework ("thinking in bets") —
arguably the single most important mental model for judging *any* decision-maker
fairly, including judging oneself.

**Fan translation:** "don't just ask if it worked out — ask if it was the right
call with what they knew at the time."

**Bow hook:** the annual report-card format (Editorial Pattern #12) should
explicitly separate "was the process sound" from "did it work out" as two
different grades, not one — teaching the distinction structurally rather than
just asserting it in prose.

---

### How these compound

These twelve are not independent — they chain into an actual decision sequence a
front office (or a well-run life) runs through:

**Asset management (1)** frames what you have → **opportunity cost (2)** and
**marginal thinking (3)** frame what any specific move actually costs →
**competitive windows (4)** and **optionality (5)** frame *when* to act or wait →
**diversification (6)**, **risk-adjusted value (8)**, and **leverage (7)** frame
*how* to structure the move → **capital allocation discipline (9)** and
**incentive design (10)** explain why organizations sometimes fail to follow their
own logic → **sunk cost discipline (11)** and **process over outcome (12)** govern
how to judge the decision, both in the moment and in hindsight.

This sequence is itself a strong candidate for a single flagship "how to think
like a front office" explainer piece or onboarding module — the twelve models
taught once, together, as a chain, rather than scattered across unrelated
articles.
